Headache After a Neck Injury? When the Neck May Be the Source

JM
Dr. Jordan Moore DC, FPSC, CICE, MACIME, CPIS
November 19, 2026
6 min read

A Headache That Started With the Injury

After a car accident, a fall, or a sports injury, many people develop a headache that was not there before. It may start at the base of the skull and wrap up to the temple or behind one eye. It may get worse when you turn your head or after a long stretch at a desk. The natural question is whether it is a migraine, a concussion symptom, or coming from the neck. All three are possible, and sometimes more than one is going on. This article explains how the neck can cause headaches and how that gets sorted out. It is educational information, not a diagnosis for any one person.

How the Neck Can Cause a Headache

The proposed explanation is anatomical. The upper three nerves of the neck (C1 to C3) supply the joints and tissues at the top of the spine. Pain fibers from those upper neck nerves converge with fibers from the trigeminal nerve, which carries sensation from the face and head, in a region of the brainstem called the trigeminocervical nucleus. Because those signals share pathways, irritation in the upper neck can be felt as pain in the forehead, temple, or around the eye. A 2019 expert consensus from the Chinese Association for the Study of Pain describes this model, and notes that the lower neck nerves are not thought to cause headache directly.

The same consensus is candid that the underlying mechanism is still poorly understood and that diagnosis and treatment remain debated. Think of it as a leading explanation, not a settled fact.

What Doctors Look For

The international headache classification, ICHD-3, defines cervicogenic headache in a careful way. In plain language, it requires:

  • A headache.
  • Clinical or imaging evidence of a disorder in the neck or its soft tissues that is known to be able to cause headache.
  • Evidence that the neck is actually responsible, shown by at least two of the following: the headache began in time with the neck problem; it improved or resolved as the neck problem did; neck motion is reduced and provocative maneuvers clearly worsen the headache; or the headache goes away after a diagnostic block of a neck structure or its nerve supply.
  • No better explanation from another headache diagnosis.

Features that may help tell it apart from migraine and tension-type headache include pain that stays on one side, provocation by pressing on neck muscles, and pain that spreads from the back of the head toward the front. Nausea and sensitivity to light and sound can also occur with a neck headache, usually milder than in migraine, so they do not rule it out.

Worth knowing: Scans can suggest a neck source but cannot prove it. The ICHD-3 points out that imaging findings in the upper neck are common in people who never have headaches.

What Else Has to Be Considered

  • Migraine and tension-type headache. These are common and can be triggered or worsened by a neck injury.
  • Concussion. Headache is one of the most common concussion symptoms. See our article on concussion after a crash with normal scans.
  • Dizziness and balance symptoms often travel with neck and head injuries. See dizziness after a neck injury.
  • Serious problems, such as bleeding, a fracture, or an injured artery, which are uncommon but must not be missed. See the red flags below.

What an Evaluation Looks Like

  • A detailed history: when the headache began relative to the injury, where it is, what makes it better or worse, and any nausea, vision changes, or numbness.
  • A neck examination: range of motion, which movements or positions reproduce the headache, and tenderness at the upper neck joints and muscles.
  • A neurological screen.
  • Imaging when the findings call for it, including flexion and extension X-rays when ligament injury or instability is a concern. See what neck instability means and how serious neck injuries are evaluated.
  • Referral to a neurologist or other specialist when the pattern does not fit a neck headache, or when a diagnostic block is being considered.

What the Treatment Evidence Shows

A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis in Chiropractic & Manual Therapies pooled 20 randomized trials with 1,439 patients with cervicogenic headache. It found that manual therapy and exercise may reduce headache intensity, frequency, and disability, and cited moderate-quality evidence supporting spinal manipulation compared with sham treatment. The same authors cautioned that most of the trials had a high risk of bias, and that when only the best-designed trials were considered, the effects were small. In short, there is real support for these approaches, but it is not as strong as marketing for them often suggests, and you should be wary of promises of a quick fix.

Red Flags: Get Care Right Away

Call 911 or go to the emergency room if a headache comes with any of the following, especially after an injury:

  • A sudden, severe headache, or the worst headache of your life
  • A headache that keeps getting worse over hours or days
  • Confusion, repeated vomiting, a seizure, or loss of consciousness
  • Double vision, slurred speech, or trouble swallowing
  • Weakness, numbness, or drooping on one side of the body or face
  • Severe neck pain with a fever or stiff neck

If you are unsure, get checked. Our article on why whiplash symptoms are often delayed explains why headaches can appear days after a crash. Our car accident care page explains how care after a collision works.

Common Questions

Can a neck injury cause headaches?

Yes, in some people. When a headache is traced to a problem in the neck, it is called a cervicogenic headache. The international headache classification (ICHD-3) requires evidence of a neck disorder that can cause headache, plus evidence that the neck is actually responsible, such as timing, reduced neck motion with headaches provoked by movement, or relief after a diagnostic nerve block. Many post-crash headaches have other causes, so a careful evaluation matters.

How is a cervicogenic headache different from a migraine or tension headache?

They can look alike, which is why they get confused. Features that point toward the neck include pain on one side that does not switch sides, a headache that starts after a neck injury, reduced neck motion, and headaches brought on by neck movement or by sustained neck positions. Nausea and light sensitivity can occur with a neck-related headache, usually milder than with migraine, so these features do not settle the question by themselves.

Do scans show a cervicogenic headache?

Not reliably. The ICHD-3 notes that imaging findings in the upper neck are common in people who do not have headaches, so they can suggest but do not prove that the neck is the cause. Imaging is still useful to rule out serious problems and to look at injured structures after trauma.

Does chiropractic care help headaches that come from the neck?

The research is encouraging but not airtight. A 2022 review of 20 trials found that manual therapy and exercise may reduce headache intensity, frequency, and disability, with moderate-quality evidence for spinal manipulation compared with sham treatment. Most of the trials were at high risk of bias, and the effects were smaller in the best-designed trials. A good clinician explains these limits and refers you when the headache does not fit a neck pattern.
