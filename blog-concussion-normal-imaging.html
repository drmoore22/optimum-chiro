Normal CT, Normal MRI, but Still Not Right After a Crash

JM
Dr. Jordan Moore DC, FPSC, CICE, MACIME, CPIS

6 min read

"Your Scans Are Normal" Is Not the Same as "You're Fine"

After a collision, many people get a CT scan in the emergency room, and some later get an MRI. The results come back normal. The doctor says there is no bleeding and no fracture, and you go home. Then days later you are still foggy, dizzy, headachy, or just not yourself, and you wonder whether something was missed.

Often nothing was missed. A scan answers one question well, and a concussion is a different question. This article explains the difference. It is educational information, not a diagnosis for any one person.

What a CT or MRI Is Actually For

In the first hours after a head injury, imaging is used to find things that need urgent treatment: bleeding in or around the brain, a skull fracture, or swelling. Those are the dangerous problems, and a normal scan is reassuring because it makes them unlikely.

A concussion, which is a mild traumatic brain injury, is mainly a change in how the brain functions, not a change that shows up as a visible tear or bleed. Pediatric guidance from the CDC, summarized for physicians in American Family Physician, states that mild traumatic brain injury is a clinical diagnosis, that CT and MRI should not be used to diagnose it, and that head CT should not be performed routinely. That guideline covers children, so it is not a rule for adults, but the underlying point carries over: the diagnosis rests on the history and examination, and a normal image does not cancel it.

Worth knowing: A normal scan tells you what you do not have: a bleed or a fracture. It does not tell you that your symptoms are imaginary, and it does not tell you what is causing them.

Symptoms That Can Follow a Concussion

  • Headache or pressure in the head
  • Dizziness or feeling off balance
  • Fogginess, slowed thinking, or trouble concentrating
  • Sensitivity to light or noise
  • Trouble sleeping, or unusual tiredness
  • Irritability, anxiety, or low mood

Most people improve over weeks. According to the CDC pediatric guideline, most symptoms resolve within one to three months, and a referral is advised if they have not resolved by four to six weeks. Again, that guidance was written for children, so adults should use it as a rough yardstick and ask their own clinician what to expect.

The Neck Is Part of the Story

In a crash, the head and neck move together. The same forces that can bruise the brain can strain the ligaments, joints, and muscles of the neck, and the symptoms of the two overlap a great deal. Headache, dizziness, and trouble concentrating can come from either one.

A small study published in the Journal of Accident and Emergency Medicine in 1995 compared 24 patients with minor head injuries to 29 with acute neck strain (whiplash). The rankings of their symptoms were correlated, and the authors concluded that head and neck injuries may be better seen as a continuum than as separate problems. Dizziness was more common in the head-injured group, while depression and fear of car travel were more common in the neck-injured group. Most of the neck-injured patients were still symptomatic at about eight weeks. This was a small study from three decades ago with different interviewers for each group, so it is a pointer and not proof, but it fits what clinicians see.

The practical lesson is that when symptoms linger after a crash, it is worth examining both the head and the neck, and not assuming that a normal head scan closes the case. For more on how neck injuries are evaluated, see how serious neck injuries are evaluated, what neck instability means, and why whiplash symptoms are often delayed.

Red Flags: Do Not Wait for These

Go to the emergency room or call 911 if, after a head injury, you have any of the following:

  • Loss of consciousness, or you cannot remember the event
  • Repeated vomiting
  • A seizure
  • A headache that is getting worse
  • Increasing confusion, drowsiness, or trouble waking
  • Weakness, numbness, slurred speech, or unequal pupils

Neck pain with new numbness, weakness, or trouble walking also needs urgent care. If you are unsure, get checked.

If Symptoms Linger

Keep a short log of what you feel, when it happens, and what makes it better or worse. Tell every clinician you see about the whole picture, including any neck pain, because symptoms that seem unrelated may be connected. If you have not improved after a few weeks, a more thorough evaluation of both the head and the neck is reasonable. Our car accident care page explains how care after a collision works.

Common Questions

If my CT and MRI are normal, can I still have a concussion?

Yes. A concussion is diagnosed from the story of the injury and your symptoms and examination, not from a scan. Pediatric guidance from the CDC states that CT and MRI should not be used to diagnose mild traumatic brain injury. Scans are used to look for bleeding or a fracture, and a normal result is good news that does not mean nothing happened.

Why do I still have headaches and dizziness after a crash?

Several things can contribute. A concussion can cause headache, dizziness and fogginess, and a neck injury can cause the same symptoms. A small study comparing head-injured and neck-injured patients found their symptoms overlapped closely, which is one reason a full evaluation looks at both the head and the neck.

How long does a concussion take to get better?

Most people recover within weeks. The CDC pediatric guideline notes that most symptoms resolve in one to three months and recommends referral if symptoms have not resolved within four to six weeks. That guideline was written for children, so adults should treat these numbers as a rough guide and ask their own clinician.

When is a head injury an emergency?

Go to the emergency room or call 911 for loss of consciousness, repeated vomiting, a seizure, a headache that keeps getting worse, worsening confusion or drowsiness, weakness or numbness, slurred speech, or unequal pupils. When in doubt, get checked right away.
