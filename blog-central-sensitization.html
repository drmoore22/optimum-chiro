Central Sensitization Explained: Why Pain Can Outlast the Injury

JM
Dr. Jordan Moore DC, FPSC, CICE, MACIME, CPIS
October 15, 2026
6 min read

When Pain and Tissue Damage Do Not Match

Pain is usually a reliable signal. A sprained ankle hurts while the ligament is healing, and the pain settles as the tissue recovers. But some people keep hurting long after the original injury has had time to heal, or hurt in ways that seem out of proportion to what imaging shows. One explanation that researchers have studied is central sensitization.

This article explains the idea in plain language. It is educational information, not a diagnosis or a recommendation for any one person.

What Central Sensitization Means

Pain is produced by the nervous system, not by tissue alone. Sensory nerves carry signals from the body to the spinal cord and brain, where they are processed and interpreted. Central sensitization describes a state in which the spinal cord and brain become more responsive to incoming signals. A helpful comparison is a volume knob turned too high: ordinary input, such as light pressure or a normal movement, can be experienced as more painful than it would be otherwise, and pain may spread beyond the original area.

Research has described signs of this heightened sensitivity in several persistent pain conditions, including some people with long-lasting whiplash-associated pain and chronic low back pain. The science is still developing, and clinicians use the term in somewhat different ways.

It Is Not "All in Your Head"

Central sensitization involves real changes in how the nervous system processes signals, and the pain is genuine. It also does not mean that nothing was ever wrong with the tissues, which is one reason a careful evaluation matters.

Features That Can Point Toward It

No single finding confirms it, but clinicians may consider it when several of the following are present:

  • Pain that has spread beyond the injured area or moves from place to place
  • Pain that seems out of proportion to the activity or touch that provokes it
  • Sensitivity to light touch, pressure, or temperature in the painful region
  • Symptoms that flare with poor sleep, stress, or activity and are slow to settle
  • Pain that has persisted well beyond the usual healing time for the original injury

These features can also occur with other problems, so they are weighed alongside your history, examination, and any imaging.

Worth knowing: Persistent pain should never simply be assumed to be sensitization. New or worsening weakness, loss of bladder or bowel control, numbness in the groin or saddle area, fever, or unexplained weight loss need prompt medical attention and are not something to wait out.

Why It Matters After an Injury

After a collision, fall, or other trauma, pain often has a real tissue source, such as an irritated joint, a strained ligament, or an injured disc. It has been proposed that continued pain signals from an unresolved source may help keep the nervous system on high alert. For that reason, an early and thorough evaluation, including a look at whether the spine is moving normally, can be useful in sorting out what is driving symptoms. Our articles on delayed whiplash symptoms and Digital Motion X-Ray describe related ideas.

How Care Is Typically Approached

When sensitization seems to be part of the picture, care usually works on several fronts at once, and no single approach is a cure:

  • Identifying and addressing any tissue source that is still contributing to pain
  • Gradual, graded activity and exercise, which research supports for many persistent pain problems
  • Education about how pain works, which can reduce fear of movement
  • Attention to sleep, stress, and general health habits
  • Hands-on care and therapies that may help calm local irritation, chosen to fit the individual
  • Coordination with other providers, such as a physician, physical therapist, or mental health professional, when that would help

Many people improve, but the course varies and no particular result can be predicted in advance.

Common Questions

Is central sensitization the same as fibromyalgia?

No. Fibromyalgia is a diagnosis made on specific criteria, and heightened nervous system sensitivity is thought to play a role in it. Sensitization is also discussed in other persistent pain problems. A clinician can explain how the terms apply to your situation.

Does a normal MRI mean my pain is not real?

No. MRI shows structure, such as discs and soft tissues, but it does not measure how sensitive the nervous system is. Pain can be real and significant with a normal scan, and many people with scan findings have no pain at all.

Can central sensitization improve?

Many people see improvement with a well-rounded plan, and the nervous system is considered adaptable. Outcomes vary, and no result can be guaranteed.

Does everyone develop it after an accident?

No. Most people recover as tissues heal. Sensitization is thought to affect some patients with persistent pain, and the factors that make it more likely are still being researched.
