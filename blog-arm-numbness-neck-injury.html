Arm Numbness After a Neck Injury? Tracing the Source

JM
Dr. Jordan Moore DC, FPSC, CICE, MACIME, CPIS
November 20, 2026
6 min read

Tingling, Numbness, or Weakness That Travels Into the Arm

After a car accident, a fall, or a sports injury, some people notice pins and needles in a hand, numbness in a few fingers, or an arm that feels weak or heavy. It is a frightening symptom, and it often sends people searching online. The neck is one possible source, but not the only one. This article explains how the neck can cause arm symptoms, how the source is traced, and when to get urgent care. It is educational information, not a diagnosis for any one person.

How a Neck Problem Reaches the Arm

The nerves that supply your arms and hands leave the spinal cord through small openings between the vertebrae in the neck. If a nerve root is compressed or irritated at that opening, the problem is called cervical radiculopathy. Common causes are a disc injury (herniation), age-related changes, and swelling after trauma. StatPearls, a physician reference, notes that disc herniation and trauma are common causes in younger adults, while degeneration and bony narrowing become more common with age.

Which nerve root is affected tends to change where you feel it. The C7 root is the most commonly affected, in more than half of cases according to StatPearls, followed by C6. As a general guide, C6 involvement is often felt in the thumb and index finger, C7 in the middle finger, and C8 in the ring and little fingers. These patterns overlap and vary from person to person, so they are clues and not a map.

What Else Can Cause Arm Numbness

Numbness in the hand does not always start in the neck, which is why an examination matters:

  • Carpal tunnel syndrome (a nerve compressed at the wrist) typically affects the thumb, index, and middle fingers and is often worse at night.
  • Ulnar nerve irritation at the elbow tends to affect the ring and little fingers, especially with a bent elbow.
  • Shoulder and upper chest problems can irritate nerves as they pass toward the arm.
  • Cord compression in the neck (myelopathy) is less common but more serious. See the red flags below.
  • Non-spine causes such as diabetes-related nerve problems can also cause numbness.

Sometimes more than one problem is present at once, which is part of why a careful evaluation matters.

How the Source Is Traced

According to the American Family Physician review of nonoperative management, the diagnosis of cervical radiculopathy can often rest on the history and physical examination alone. A good exam looks for:

  • Strength in specific muscle groups tied to each nerve root, such as the deltoid and biceps (C5), wrist extensors (C6), triceps (C7), and finger flexors (C8).
  • Sensation along the pattern of each root.
  • Reflexes, which can be reduced or uneven when a root is affected.
  • Provocative tests, such as the Spurling test, where the neck is extended and rotated toward the symptomatic side, and the upper limb tension test. Both help raise or lower suspicion of a nerve root problem. In a widely cited 2003 study, a cluster of four findings (a positive upper limb tension test, a positive Spurling test, a positive distraction test, and limited rotation toward the involved side) made radiculopathy substantially more likely when three or four were present.
  • Neck movement: pain or tingling that is reproduced by extending the neck or turning toward the affected side points toward the neck.
  • Signs of cord involvement, such as hand clumsiness, trouble with balance when walking, or exaggerated reflexes.

Worth knowing: An abnormal MRI does not automatically explain your symptoms. The American Family Physician review notes that about 57 percent of people over 64 with no symptoms have a disc herniation on MRI, so images have to be matched to the exam.

When Imaging Helps

The same review suggests MRI after four to six weeks without improvement, when deficits are progressing, or when something more serious such as cord compression is suspected. X-rays are useful after trauma, and flexion and extension views can look for instability when ligament injury is a concern. See what neck instability means and how serious neck injuries are evaluated. When the picture is unclear, nerve conduction testing or a specialist's input may help sort out whether the problem is in the neck or elsewhere along the nerve.

What to Expect: Most People Improve

The outlook is generally good. StatPearls reports that over 85 percent of acute cervical radiculopathy resolves within 8 to 12 weeks without any specific treatment, and about 83 percent of patients have satisfactory function at three years. The American Family Physician review reports about 88 percent improving within four weeks of nonoperative management. Factors linked to poorer outcomes include symptoms lasting over three months, high baseline disability, smoking, and heavy physical work.

For treatment, the American Family Physician review found low-quality evidence supporting a supervised program of strengthening and stretching for the neck, shoulder, and upper arm in the acute phase. Evidence for collars and traction is mixed. Epidural steroid injections have good-quality evidence for radiculopathy caused by a disc herniation in people who have not improved with other care, and surgical referral is considered when there is no improvement after roughly four to eight weeks, or sooner for progressive weakness. Progressive weakness or new deficits should prompt re-evaluation and, if needed, referral to a spine surgeon.

Red Flags: Get Care Right Away

Call 911 or go to the emergency room, or seek same-day care, for any of the following:

  • Numbness or weakness in both arms, or in an arm and a leg
  • Trouble walking, loss of balance, or new hand clumsiness
  • Loss of bladder or bowel control
  • Rapidly worsening weakness
  • Numbness with chest pain, trouble speaking, facial drooping, or sudden severe headache
  • Fever, unexplained weight loss, or severe pain at rest with neck pain

Common Questions

Can a neck injury cause numbness or tingling in the arm?

Yes. When a nerve root in the neck is compressed or irritated, often by a disc injury or swelling around the nerve, it can cause pain, numbness, tingling, or weakness that travels into the arm. This is called cervical radiculopathy. Arm and hand numbness has other possible causes as well, such as nerve compression at the wrist or elbow, so the pattern and an examination matter.

Will a pinched nerve in the neck heal on its own?

Often, yes. Physician reviews report that most acute cervical radiculopathy improves without surgery. One reference states that over 85 percent resolves within 8 to 12 weeks, and a review in American Family Physician reports about 88 percent improving within four weeks of nonoperative care. Individual results vary, and worsening weakness or new deficits are reasons to be re-evaluated promptly.

Do I need an MRI for arm numbness after a crash?

Not always right away. Guidance in American Family Physician suggests MRI when there is no improvement after four to six weeks, when deficits are progressing, or when something more serious is suspected. Disc findings on MRI are common in people with no symptoms, so an image needs to be matched to your exam. Flexion and extension X-rays may be used after trauma when ligament instability is a concern.

When is arm numbness an emergency?

Seek emergency care for numbness or weakness in both arms or legs, trouble walking or hand clumsiness, loss of bladder or bowel control, or rapidly worsening weakness. Also seek urgent care for numbness with chest pain, trouble speaking, or facial drooping, which can signal a heart or stroke emergency rather than a neck problem.
