Dizzy After a Neck Injury? What May Be Behind It

JM
Dr. Jordan Moore DC, FPSC, CICE, MACIME, CPIS
November 12, 2026
6 min read

Dizzy Since the Injury, and Nobody Can Say Why

After a car accident, a fall, or a hard hit, some people notice that the room feels unsteady, their head feels light, or they get dizzy when they turn their neck. The first question is usually whether the neck is to blame. The honest answer is that sometimes it may be, but dizziness after an injury has several possible sources, and sorting them out is the real work. This article explains how that sorting works. It is educational information, not a diagnosis for any one person.

"Dizzy" Can Mean Different Things

  • Light-headedness: a woozy, faint, or floating feeling.
  • Unsteadiness: feeling off balance, especially when walking or turning.
  • Vertigo: a sense that you or the room is spinning or moving.

Which of these you feel, when it starts, how long it lasts, and what brings it on are all clues. The Bárány Society, an international group that defines how dizziness disorders are classified, notes that dizziness, more than true spinning vertigo, is the usual complaint in people whose symptoms are thought to come from the neck.

Why the Neck Might Be Involved

The leading proposed explanation is called the sensory mismatch theory. The joints and muscles of the upper neck are packed with position sensors (proprioceptors) that tell the brain where the head is relative to the body. That information is combined with signals from the inner ear and the eyes, in part at the vestibular nuclei, which are balance-processing centers in the brainstem. If an injury, muscle spasm, or pain changes the signals coming from the neck, they may no longer agree with what the inner ear and eyes report, and the brain may interpret that conflict as dizziness or unsteadiness. A 2022 review calls this the most convincing explanation proposed, and a 2025 review in Frontiers in Neurology describes the same model.

Both reviews are also candid that this is a theory, not a proven mechanism. Much of the supporting work comes from animal studies and small human studies, there is no gold-standard test, and most people with neck pain never become dizzy, so other factors must be involved. A position paper from the Bárány Society classification committee concluded that evidence for a direct mechanistic link between neck problems and dizziness is lacking, and for that reason it prefers the label "cervical dizziness" over the older "cervicogenic dizziness."

The committee describes three features that show up consistently in the literature:

  • Neck stiffness and pain get worse with neck movement.
  • Neck movements trigger brief imbalance, light-headedness, or a sense of movement.
  • Treatment aimed at the neck improves the neck pain, stiffness, and dizziness.

The same paper declines to propose formal diagnostic criteria for everyday clinical use, which tells you how unsettled the science still is.

Worth knowing: Dizziness that happens with no neck pain, that occurs even when your head and neck are still, or that only happens when you change position (like rolling over in bed) points away from the neck as the main source.

What Else Has to Be Ruled Out

Because neck-related dizziness is hard to prove, it is treated largely as a diagnosis of exclusion. Other causes come first:

  • BPPV. Tiny crystals in the inner ear shift out of place and cause brief spinning with head position changes. It can follow a head injury and is treatable.
  • Vestibular migraine. The Bárány paper identifies migraine as the most common cause of combined neck pain and dizziness, so it has to be considered.
  • Concussion. The same forces that strain the neck can injure the brain. In a small 1995 study, dizziness was reported by about 46 percent of head-injured patients and about 21 percent of patients with whiplash-type neck strain, which shows that dizziness is common after either. See our article on concussion after a crash with normal scans.
  • Blood vessel or brain problems. Rarely, an injury to an artery in the neck or a stroke can cause dizziness. These are the dangerous causes that the Bárány paper says must be considered, and they are why red flags matter.

There is also a chicken-and-egg problem. Any condition that makes you dizzy can make you hold your head still, and a stiff neck can follow. So a stiff neck alongside dizziness does not by itself prove the neck is the cause.

How an Evaluation Sorts It Out

A review of cervicogenic dizziness in the Journal of Orthopaedic and Sports Physical Therapy describes the diagnosis as depending on connecting the dizziness and balance problems with neck pain, while ruling out other vestibular disorders through the history, an examination, and vestibular testing. In practice, a careful evaluation after a neck injury usually includes:

  • A detailed history: when it started, what triggers it, how long episodes last, and any headaches, hearing changes, or vision changes.
  • A neck examination, including how motion reproduces or changes the symptoms.
  • A neurological screen, including eye movements and balance.
  • Imaging when the findings call for it, and referral to a neurologist, ear specialist, or vestibular therapist when the pattern does not fit the neck. For more on how serious neck injuries are evaluated, see our neck injury evaluation article and our article on neck instability.

What the Treatment Evidence Shows

The same review describes treatment for neck-related dizziness as a combination of manual therapy and vestibular rehabilitation, which is exercise-based retraining of balance and eye-head coordination. But the evidence is weaker than the confident marketing around this topic suggests. The Bárány paper judges the available trials weak, and notes that in the best-powered trial the early benefit did not hold at 12 months. It also cautions that improvement after treatment alone cannot prove the neck was the cause.

The practical takeaway is to expect a careful look at the whole picture, to be wary of anyone who promises that fixing the neck will fix the dizziness, and to expect a referral if the evaluation points elsewhere.

Red Flags: Get Care Right Away

Call 911 or go to the emergency room if dizziness comes with any of the following, especially after an injury:

  • A sudden, severe headache or severe neck pain
  • Double vision or sudden vision loss
  • Slurred speech or trouble swallowing
  • Numbness, drooping, or weakness in the face, arm, or leg
  • Inability to walk or stand
  • Fainting or loss of consciousness

Common Questions

Can a neck injury cause dizziness?

It may, but the link is less settled than many people assume. A Bárány Society committee, which sets international standards for dizziness diagnosis, uses the term cervical dizziness because the mechanism connecting the neck to dizziness has not been proven. Neck-related dizziness is usually described as light-headedness or unsteadiness that comes with neck pain and is triggered by neck movement, and other causes need to be ruled out first.

What else can cause dizziness after a crash?

Common alternatives include benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV, an inner ear problem), vestibular migraine, and concussion. Rarely, dizziness signals a more serious problem such as an injury to an artery in the neck or a stroke, which is why new or severe symptoms need prompt medical attention.

Should I see a chiropractor, a neurologist or an ear specialist for dizziness?

It depends on the pattern. Dizziness that comes with neck pain and neck movement, after a neck injury, is reasonable to have examined by a clinician who evaluates spinal injuries. If the picture suggests an inner ear, migraine or brain cause, or red flags are present, a referral to the right specialist or emergency care comes first. A good evaluation should tell you which path fits.

When is dizziness an emergency?

Call 911 or go to the emergency room for sudden severe dizziness with a new severe headache or neck pain, double vision, slurred speech, trouble swallowing, facial numbness or drooping, weakness or numbness on one side, inability to walk, or loss of consciousness. After a significant injury, do not wait to see if it passes.
