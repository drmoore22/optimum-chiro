Disc, Facet or SI Joint? Finding the Source of Low Back Pain

JM
Dr. Jordan Moore DC, FPSC, CICE, MACIME, CPIS
November 2, 2026
7 min read

"Low Back Pain" Is Not a Diagnosis

When your back hurts, the label "low back pain" only says where. It does not say what is hurting. Three structures account for a large share of chronic low back pain: the intervertebral disc, the small facet joints at the back of the spine, and the sacroiliac (SI) joint, where the spine meets the pelvis. They can feel alike, and they respond best to different approaches, so telling them apart is worth the effort.

This article explains the typical patterns, what the research says about how reliably they can be told apart, and why no single test settles it. It is educational information, not a diagnosis for any one person.

The Three Common Sources

  • The disc. The discs between the vertebrae are cushions with a tough outer ring. Small tears in that ring (annular fissures), bulges, and herniations can all be painful, and the outer ring has a nerve supply of its own.
  • The facet joints. These paired joints guide and limit spinal motion. Like any joint, they can become irritated or inflamed.
  • The SI joint. This joint transfers load between the spine and the pelvis, and it can be a pain source on its own.

How common is each? A review from a university spine center looked at 157 cases in which the source was confirmed with diagnostic procedures such as discography and injections. About 43 percent were from the disc, 31 percent from the facet joints, and 18 percent from the SI joint, with the rest from other sources. Those patients had chronic pain and were referred to a specialty center, so the numbers do not describe everyone with a sore back. The same study found that the average age differed by source: about 44 years for disc pain, and about 60 and 62 for facet and SI joint pain.

Typical Patterns, Not Rules

Clinicians listen for patterns in how the pain behaves. These are commonly described tendencies, and any one of them can be absent or misleading.

  • Disc pattern: pain that is worse with sitting, bending forward, coughing or sneezing, and that may be felt in the center of the back. It can be hard to stand up straight after sitting.
  • Facet pattern: pain that is worse with leaning backward or twisting, often one-sided, and often better when bending forward or lying down. It tends to stay above the knee.
  • SI joint pattern: pain low on one side, near the dimple above the buttock, often worse with stairs, rolling over in bed, standing on one leg, or getting up from a chair.

Pain with leg symptoms below the knee, numbness, or weakness can point toward irritation of a nerve root, which is a separate question from which structure is the source.

Worth knowing: Age is one clue, not an answer. Disc pain was more common in younger adults in the review above, and facet and SI joint pain in older ones, but there is a lot of overlap, and a person can have more than one source at the same time.

What the Research Says About Telling Them Apart

A systematic review in the European Spine Journal (2007) pooled 41 studies of tests meant to identify the disc, SI joint, or facet joints as the source of pain. Its findings are a useful reality check:

  • Some tests can shift the probability that the disc or the SI joint is the source, but the shifts were "usually small and at best moderate."
  • Centralization, where pain moves from the leg or buttock toward the center of the spine during repeated movements, was the only clinical finding that increased the likelihood of a disc source.
  • Single manual tests for the SI joint were uninformative, but combinations of tests were informative.
  • None of the tests for facet joint pain were found to be informative.

A separate study of 48 patients tested a cluster of six SI joint provocation tests against an injection into the joint. Having three or more positive tests identified SI joint pain with 94 percent sensitivity and 78 percent specificity. That is a small study done by physical therapists in one setting, so it supports the idea of reading tests together, not relying on one.

The takeaway is not that the sorting is hopeless. It is that the answer comes from putting together the history, a careful hands-on examination, repeated-movement testing, and imaging, and that honest clinicians hold their conclusions with some humility.

Where Imaging Fits

X-ray and MRI are valuable, but they show structure, not pain. The same review found that some MRI features made a disc source more likely, and that the absence of disc degeneration on MRI made it less likely. But disc and joint changes are also common in people who have no back pain at all, so an abnormal-looking scan does not prove that finding is the cause. Imaging is most useful when it is read alongside the examination and used to rule out more serious problems.

Why Bother Sorting It Out?

A clearer picture of the likely source helps decide what to try first, when to image, and when to refer for a specialist opinion. It also helps avoid treating one structure when another is the problem. In fairness, the research is still unclear about how much source-specific treatment improves outcomes, so the goal here is a sensible plan, not a guarantee.

When Back Pain Follows an Accident

Low back pain after a car accident deserves the same careful approach, and it is often one of several areas hurt at once. Pain can start hours or days later, so watch how it develops. You can read about how care after a collision works on our car accident care page, and about why symptoms are often delayed.

Red Flags That Need Urgent Care

  • Loss of bladder or bowel control
  • Numbness in the groin or saddle area
  • Progressive weakness in a leg, or trouble walking
  • Fever with back pain, or back pain with a history of cancer
  • Back pain after significant trauma

If any of these apply, seek emergency care first.

Common Questions

Can an MRI tell me whether my back pain is from the disc, a facet joint or the SI joint?

Not by itself. An MRI shows structure, and some findings make a disc source more likely, but many people without back pain also have disc and joint changes on MRI. The source is usually worked out by combining your history, a hands-on examination, and imaging, and in research settings it is confirmed with targeted injections.

Is the SI joint a common cause of low back pain?

It is one of the three most common sources studied. In one university spine center review of patients who had diagnostic injections, about 18 percent had sacroiliac joint pain, compared with about 43 percent for the disc and 31 percent for the facet joints. These figures come from a selected group of chronic pain patients, so they are not rates for everyone with a sore back.

What does it mean if my pain moves toward the middle of my back with certain movements?

Pain that moves from the leg or buttock toward the center of the spine with repeated movements is called centralization. In a systematic review it was the only clinical finding that increased the likelihood of a disc source, although the increase was modest.

When should I get low back pain checked urgently?

Seek emergency care for loss of bladder or bowel control, numbness in the groin or saddle area, progressive leg weakness, fever with back pain, or back pain after significant trauma. Call 911 or go to the nearest emergency room.
