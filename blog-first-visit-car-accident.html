What to Expect at Your First Visit After a Car Accident

JM
Dr. Jordan Moore DC, FPSC, CICE, MACIME, CPIS
November 26, 2026
6 min read

Knowing What Happens Can Make the First Visit Easier

If you were just in a car accident, walking into a new office can feel uncertain, especially if you are sore, shaken, or unsure whether you even "need" to be seen. This article walks through what a thorough first visit after a collision generally looks like, step by step, so you know what to expect. It is general educational information. Every patient and every office is a little different.

Before You Go

  • Go to the emergency room first if there are warning signs. Severe or worsening headache, weakness or numbness in both arms or legs, trouble walking, loss of bladder or bowel control, chest pain, confusion, repeated vomiting, or trouble speaking all need emergency care, not an office visit.
  • Do not wait for symptoms to get bad. Pain and stiffness often show up a day or more later. See why whiplash symptoms are often delayed.
  • Bring what you have. Photo ID, insurance and claim details, ER or imaging records, a medication list, and notes on the crash. Missing paperwork should never delay care.

Step 1: Your Story

A good evaluation starts with listening. Expect questions about how the crash happened: the direction of impact, your approximate speed, whether you were wearing a seatbelt, where your head and hands were, and whether you hit anything inside the vehicle. These details help explain which structures were most likely stressed. You will also be asked about every symptom you have, not just the one that bothers you most, including headache, dizziness, arm or leg symptoms, jaw pain, sleep, concentration, and mood. Past injuries, prior neck or back problems, and your health history matter too, because they change how findings are interpreted. Our article on what a post-accident evaluation includes covers this part in more detail.

Step 2: The Physical Examination

The exam is hands-on and focused on what the crash may have affected. It typically includes:

  • Observation and posture, including how you move and whether you guard any area.
  • Range of motion of the neck and back, noting where movement is limited or reproduces symptoms.
  • Palpation to find tender, tight, or swollen areas.
  • Orthopedic tests that stress specific joints and tissues.
  • A neurological screen of strength, sensation, and reflexes, which helps tell a muscle and joint problem from a nerve problem. See how arm numbness after a neck injury is traced.
  • Screening for concussion and red flags, because head and neck injuries often occur together. See why a concussion can have normal imaging.

Step 3: Deciding Whether Imaging Is Needed

Imaging is a decision, not a reflex. Your history and exam findings guide whether X-rays, advanced imaging such as MRI, or no imaging is appropriate. If you have already had ER imaging, bring it so it can be reviewed. A normal X-ray does not rule out every injury, because X-rays show bone and not ligaments, discs, or nerves. For that reason, a normal ER film does not always settle the question. Read more about how serious neck injuries are evaluated and what neck instability means.

Step 4: What the Findings Mean

After the exam, you should be told what was found in plain language. Clinicians often describe whiplash-associated disorders using the Quebec Task Force classification, published in 1995 (Spitzer et al., Spine):

  • Grade 0: no neck complaint and no physical signs.
  • Grade I: neck complaint only (pain, stiffness, or tenderness) with no physical signs.
  • Grade II: neck complaint plus musculoskeletal signs, such as decreased range of motion or point tenderness.
  • Grade III: neck complaint plus neurological signs, such as decreased reflexes, weakness, or sensory deficits.
  • Grade IV: neck complaint plus fracture or dislocation.

Worth knowing: Under this classification, symptoms such as dizziness, tinnitus, headache, memory loss, trouble swallowing, and jaw pain can occur in any grade. They are not a sign that you are exaggerating, and they are worth mentioning at your visit. See dizziness after a neck injury and headache after a neck injury.

Step 5: The Plan and the Follow-Up

The visit should end with a clear clinical impression and a plan: what care is recommended, how often you will be reassessed, what improvement should look like, and what would trigger a referral, additional imaging, or a second opinion. Chiropractic care does not replace emergency, medical, or surgical care when those are needed, and knowing when to refer is part of a good evaluation. Your records should accurately document your findings and progress. Common Questions

How soon after a car accident should I be seen?

As soon as you reasonably can. Whiplash-type symptoms are often delayed, and an early baseline exam documents what is present while the event is fresh. If you have any emergency warning signs, such as severe headache, weakness, loss of bladder or bowel control, chest pain, or trouble speaking, go to the emergency room first.

Do I need X-rays or an MRI at the first visit?

Not automatically. Whether imaging is needed depends on your history and examination findings. Some people need X-rays right away, some need advanced imaging later if they are not improving or if neurological signs are present, and some need no imaging at all. The reasoning should be explained to you.

What should I bring to my first visit?

Bring photo ID, your insurance information, the claim or adjuster details if you have them, any ER, urgent care, or imaging records, a list of medications, and your own notes about the crash and your symptoms. If you have photos of the vehicles, bring them too. If something is missing, do not delay care to find it.

Does a first visit mean I am committing to a long course of care?

No. A first visit is an evaluation. You should leave knowing what was found, what it means, what the plan is, and what would change the plan, so you can make your own informed decision.
